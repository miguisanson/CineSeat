Seven modules taught you seven capabilities in isolation. This chapter is about what happens when you assemble them into one coherent product — because that's a separate skill, and it's the one interviews probe.

Here is what you will be able to do by the end of this video.

It started as one sentence: a cinema ticket-booking app where you browse films, pick a schedule, choose seats, see the price and confirm a reservation.

That became a requirements list — deliberately unglamorous. Movie list. Movie details. Showtimes. Seat selection. Booking summary. Confirmation. A bookings history. Cancellation. Validation alerts. A list a non-developer could have written.

And one thing was missing, which an early review caught: there was no profile screen. Adding accounts turned out to unlock roughly a third of everything else — bookings could be owned, tickets shared, reviews gated on whether you held a ticket.

That's the strongest argument for writing requirements down where somebody else can read and question them, rather than holding them in your head.

The requirements became this flowchart. Rectangles are processes, diamonds are decisions.

Trace the happy path first — start, browse, select, confirm, done. Now look at everything else. Not signed in, routing off to account creation. No seat selected, routing to a validation alert. Cancel, which releases the seats and cancels the pending reminder.

Most of real app design is the handling of things going wrong. Those branches are where bugs breed, and drawing them before any code existed is why they were handled deliberately rather than discovered later.

Then a wireframe — rough sketches deciding only what goes where, not what it looks like. Chasing pixel-perfect visuals at that stage wastes effort on decisions that change once real content arrives.

Two things changed between plan and build, and both are healthy. The 'how to use' tab was cut — it existed only to fill a fourth tab, and Settings made it redundant. And a payment step was scoped out on purpose: better to ship no payment than a fake one that misleads.

Here's the result. Five taps from browsing a film to a confirmed, locally-persisted ticket with its own booking ID.

And every step is a module doing real work. The detail screen is Module two. The data is Modules four and five. The structure holding it together is Module six.

One design detail deserves its own moment, because it's the reuse the architecture module was building toward. Movies book by seat. Concerts and seminars book by quantity.

Rather than two separate booking systems, there's a single reservation core with two different front-ends. That's exactly the reuse Clean Architecture and protocol-based injection are supposed to make possible — and here it is, on screen, working.
