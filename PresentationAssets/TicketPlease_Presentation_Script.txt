[Slide 6 — Technology snapshot]  *** worth slowing down here — explain MVVM ***
Let me pause on the tech, because it's the backbone of the whole app. It's built with Swift and UIKit — Apple's mature, proven tools.

The important part is the architecture: it's built on MVVM, which stands for Model–View–ViewModel. It sounds technical, but the idea is simple — you split the app into three jobs:

  • The Model is just the data — a movie, a booking, a seat. Plain information, no logic.
  • The View is what you see on screen — the buttons, the lists, the seat map. Its only job is to display things and pass along your taps. It doesn't do any of the thinking.
  • The ViewModel sits in the middle — it's the brain for each screen. It takes the raw data, decides how it should look (like turning a price into "₱350.00" or a rating into "4.7 / 5.0"), and hands that to the View. It also handles what happens when you tap something.

Why split it up like that? Because each piece stays simple and focused, and the screen code doesn't get tangled up with the data logic. In this app, every feature has its own ViewModel — one for movies, one for bookings, one for the profile, and so on. So if I need to change how bookings work, I just go to the booking ViewModel, and the rest of the app doesn't care. It makes everything easier to test, easier to fix, and easier for someone else to jump into.

On top of MVVM I added Clean Architecture and dependency injection — which is really just a fancy way of saying the data layer is kept separate too, and everything gets wired together in one place. The practical payoff: I could swap the local files for a real backend later without rewriting the screens. And one more thing — passwords go into the iOS Keychain, the secure encrypted area, and there are no third-party libraries at all. For client work that's a real plus: fewer outside libraries means fewer security and maintenance headaches later.

[Slide 7 — The four tabs]

SECTION 3 — FEATURE DEEP-DIVES  —  [Slides 25–30]
==========================================================

[Slide 25 — section title.]

[Slide 26 — Booking & seat maps]
Let me go a little deeper on a few features. First, the seat maps. Each cinema has its own layout — rows, aisles, VIP versus standard. When you pick seats, it knows what's already taken and blocks it. Every booking gets a traceable ID, and pricing is in pesos — 350 for standard, 550 for VIP. The point: this same engine could model any reservation — seats, desks, rooms, time slots.

[Slide 27 — The review system]
The reviews have real rules. You can read reviews anywhere, but you can only write one after you've actually booked something and the showtime has passed — so no fake reviews. One review per person per item, and you can edit or delete your own. I also kept the app's own reviews completely separate from online movie reviews pulled from a public database, so they never get mixed up. For a client, that's basically a moderated feedback system.

[Slide 28 — Locations & maps]
The Locations map has live pins for every venue. Tap one, you get its details and showtimes, and you can jump straight into booking or open it in Apple Maps. Same pattern you'd use for a branch locator or an ATM finder.

[Slide 29 — Notifications]
The app also sends reminders — here they are as a banner at the top and on the lock screen. These are scheduled right on the device, no server needed. For a real product you'd move these to a server, but it's the same mechanism you'd use for appointment or payment alerts.

[Slide 30 — Persistence & security]
And quickly on data — everything has a proper place. Bookings, profiles, and reviews are saved as files; settings in their own store; and, importantly, passwords go in the iOS Keychain, the secure encrypted area. Nothing sensitive is stored in plain text.
